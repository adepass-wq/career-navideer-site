CAREER EXPLORATION

Why lifestyle belongs in the career conversation

A career is not chosen in isolation. Work supports a life, and that life should be part of the decision from the beginning.

Anthony DePass, Ph.D.Lifestyle-based career planning
Back to Insights

“What do you want to be?” sounds like a simple question. It is also incomplete. People do not experience a career as a title alone. They experience the schedule, income, location, training, responsibilities, culture and possibilities that come with it.

Housing, transportation, family goals, geographic preferences, financial expectations, work values and time outside work all shape whether a pathway is sustainable. When career exploration ignores those factors, learners can become attached to titles without understanding the lives those careers may support or require.

Begin with the life, not the label

Starting with lifestyle changes the conversation. Instead of demanding an immediate occupational answer, it invites learners to imagine the conditions under which they hope to live and work. What matters to them? What responsibilities are likely to shape their choices? What level of income might their preferred lifestyle require? What tradeoffs are they willing to make?

These questions do not reduce career decisions to money. They place income alongside values, family, community, meaning and opportunity. The purpose is not to shrink aspiration to a budget. It is to help aspiration meet reality while there is still room to explore.

Lifestyle is not an extra consideration after a career is chosen. It is part of what makes a career choice meaningful.

Make tradeoffs visible

Every path contains tradeoffs. A career may offer high earnings but require extensive education, geographic mobility or an unpredictable schedule. Another may align closely with a learner’s interests while offering a different income trajectory. A third may provide a faster route into work and room for later advancement.

When those differences are visible, the learner can ask better questions. Is the preparation realistic? Does the work environment fit? Are there related occupations that preserve what is appealing while changing the tradeoffs? What could be developed now to keep several options open?

Why this matters across life stages

For younger learners

Lifestyle questions make an unfamiliar future concrete. They help students see why education, earnings and work conditions matter without forcing premature commitment.

For college and CTE learners

Lifestyle provides a lens for comparing pathways that differ in cost, duration, credential requirements and employment settings. It encourages learners to investigate the work rather than rely on prestige or familiarity.

For adults

Adults often face immediate constraints: income continuity, caregiving, location and time available for training. Lifestyle-based planning acknowledges those realities while helping them recognize transferable knowledge, abilities and skills.

From aspiration to action

A useful career tool should connect the desired life to an income lens, relevant occupations, education options and a development path. It should also allow people to return as priorities change. Career decisions are not permanent declarations; they are choices made with the best available understanding at a particular point in life.

Better planning therefore does two things at once: it respects the learner’s sense of possibility and gives that possibility enough structure to become actionable.
