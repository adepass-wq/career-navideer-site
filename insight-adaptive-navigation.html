WORKFORCE CHANGE

Career navigation must become more adaptive

Technology, skills-based hiring and new credential routes are changing how people enter and move through work. Career guidance must change with them.

Anthony DePass, Ph.D.Workforce pathways and transition
Back to Insights

The traditional image of a career pathway is linear: choose a field, earn a degree, enter an occupation and advance. That route remains valuable for many people, but it no longer describes the full landscape of work.

Individuals enter careers through certificates, apprenticeships, two- and four-year programs, military experience, employer training, professional credentials and advanced study. They move between industries, combine roles and return to education at different points in life. Technology changes tasks, while skills-based hiring creates new ways to demonstrate readiness.

One route cannot serve every learner

A high-school student discovering unfamiliar occupations, a CTE learner comparing credentials and an experienced adult seeking a transition do not arrive with the same question.

Adaptive navigation begins by meeting each person at their actual starting point. Some need broad exposure. Some know a job title and want to understand the work. Others need to translate experience into new possibilities without discarding what they have already built.

Career navigation should help people use what they have, understand what is changing and see more than one credible route forward.

Connect four kinds of information

The work

People need a realistic view of tasks, tools, work context, values and preparation, not only a short description or salary figure.

The person

Interests matter, but so do existing knowledge, skills and abilities, education, constraints, values and desired lifestyle. These factors should inform exploration without becoming restrictive labels.

The market

Earnings, outlook and industry demand provide context. They should be presented with dates, sources and appropriate limitations rather than treated as guarantees.

The route

Users need to see the programs, credentials, experiences and development activities that can move them from interest toward readiness.

Transition is an asset-mapping problem

Career changers are too often asked to begin again. A better approach starts by identifying what can transfer. Knowledge is what a person understands. Abilities are enduring capacities used across tasks. Skills are learned proficiencies developed through education, work and life.

Mapping these assets against a new occupation can reveal strengths, gaps and realistic development priorities. The result is more useful than a generic list of recommended jobs because it explains why a pathway may fit and what work remains.

Adaptation requires continuity

A career tool becomes more valuable when people can save sessions, revisit earlier choices and update their path. A decision made today may change after a workplace experience, course, conversation or shift in family needs. The platform should preserve that learning rather than force the user to begin again.

Adaptive navigation is not about chasing every new trend. It is about creating a durable process: understand the person, investigate the work, compare routes, develop what is needed and return when conditions change.
