CAREER DECISION INTELLIGENCE

Track decisions, not just clicks

The most useful signal is not that a learner opened a page. It is how questions, comparisons and next steps develop over time.

Anthony DePass, Ph.D.Decision behavior and longitudinal learning
Back to Insights

Most digital career platforms can report that someone logged in, completed an assessment or viewed an occupation. Those measures help us understand reach and basic engagement, but they say little about how a career decision is taking shape.

Career decision-making is rarely a single event. A learner may begin by expressing a preferred lifestyle, explore unfamiliar occupations, revisit a possibility, compare preparation requirements, narrow a direction and investigate an educational route. Those actions form a sequence, and that sequence is more informative than any isolated click.

From activity counts to a decision journey

A page view tells us that something was seen. A decision journey asks what happened before and after that view. Did exploration broaden? Did the learner return? Did they compare several possibilities or investigate one in greater depth? Did they connect a career to education and to the knowledge, abilities and skills required?

This is the basis of Career Decision Intelligence, or CDI: the disciplined use of observable exploration patterns to understand how people move through career decisions and where support may be useful.

CDI does not read a learner’s mind. It makes the visible parts of a decision journey easier to understand.

Four patterns worth observing

Expression

Learners reveal priorities through choices about lifestyle, work values, interests, education and supplemental questions. These choices provide context, not a permanent label.

Exploration

The range and variety of occupations viewed can show whether a learner is discovering broadly, testing a hypothesis or remaining within a familiar set of options.

Narrowing

Repeated comparisons, saved options and deeper investigation may indicate that possibilities are becoming more concrete. Narrowing is not automatically progress; it should be interpreted alongside the learner’s own account.

Investigation

Attention to preparation, tasks, work context, credentials and knowledge, abilities and skills can signal a shift from curiosity toward practical evaluation.

Why longitudinal information matters

A single session captures a moment. Multiple sessions can reveal change. A learner who explores widely in September may return in November with a focused question. An adult may revisit a transition after considering family responsibilities or training time. A student may reject an occupation after learning what the work involves, which can be as valuable as selecting one.

Longitudinal information lets educators and workforce programs support the process instead of waiting only for an endpoint. It can help identify where learners need exposure, clarification, a mentor conversation or a concrete next step.

Evidence needs boundaries

Behavioral data must be interpreted carefully. An observed pattern is not a psychological diagnosis, proof of motivation or prediction of success. Small samples should be described rather than treated as a basis for broad causal claims. Survey responses, usage patterns and interviews are strongest when considered together.

The purpose of CDI is not surveillance or classification. Its value is practical: making career development visible enough that learners can reflect on their journey and institutions can provide more timely, relevant support.
